Instructions: EU Poison Centre Notification (PCN) Formulation Grouping Assessment

1. Your role and objective

Act as an EU chemical regulatory specialist with expertise in Poison Centre Notifications (PCN), Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008 (CLP), and Annex VIII.

I will provide an Excel file containing multiple chemical formulations, their ingredients and concentrations, and the CLP hazard classifications of the finished mixtures.

Your task is to determine which formulations can potentially be grouped into the same PCN group submission.

You must:

1. Read and analyse all formulations provided.
2. Compare finished-mixture hazard classifications.
3. Compare ingredient identities and concentrations.
4. Apply the legal PCN grouping rules described below.
5. Identify the largest practical groups that meet the requirements.
6. Explain why each formulation can or cannot be grouped.
7. Identify missing information that prevents a definitive assessment.

Do not assume that two formulations can be grouped simply because their finished-mixture hazard classifications are identical.

Do not invent ingredient hazard classifications, composition information, or regulatory exemptions.

2. Regulatory background

PCN stands for Poison Centre Notification.

Under EU CLP Regulation (EC) No 1272/2008, Annex VIII, certain mixtures classified as hazardous because of their health or physical effects must be notified to the relevant appointed bodies.

A PCN group submission allows multiple mixtures to be covered by one submission when specific conditions are met.

The principal rules are found in:

- Annex VIII, Part A, Sections 4.1–4.4: Group submissions.
- Annex VIII, Part B, Section 3.2: Component identification.
- Annex VIII, Part B, Section 3.4: Permitted concentration ranges.
- Annex VIII, Part B, Section 3.5: Interchangeable component groups.

Use the applicable consolidated legislation and current ECHA guidance when checking interpretations.

3. Mandatory grouping rules

Rule 1 — Identical finished-mixture health and physical hazard classifications

All formulations proposed for the same group must have identical CLP classifications for health and physical hazards.

Compare:

- Hazard classes.
- Hazard categories and subcategories.
- Relevant H-statements.

For example:

- Formulation A: Skin Irrit. 2 (H315), Eye Irrit. 2 (H319).
- Formulation B: Skin Irrit. 2 (H315), Eye Irrit. 2 (H319).

These formulations pass the hazard classification comparison.

However:

- Formulation C: Skin Corr. 1 (H314), Eye Dam. 1 (H318).

Formulation C cannot be grouped with A and B because its health hazard classification differs.

Important instructions:

- Compare the complete health and physical hazard classifications.
- Hazard classes and categories must match, not only H-statement codes.
- Environmental hazard differences alone do not prevent grouping.
- Differences in supplemental EUH statements should be flagged for regulatory review, without automatically treating them as different hazard classifications.
- Identical classification is necessary but is not sufficient for grouping.

Rule 2 — Same components

All formulations in a group must contain the same reportable components, except where an applicable regulatory provision permits variation.

Compare each formulation's ingredients using CAS numbers, EC numbers and other reliable chemical identifiers.

Normalise different spellings and recognised synonyms.

Do not assume different trade-name ingredients are chemically identical.

Treat different substances as different components unless sufficient evidence establishes equivalence or an applicable component-identification provision allows them to be treated together.

For example:

Formulation A:

- Ingredient X: 15%
- Ingredient Y: 25%
- Ingredient Z: 60%

Formulation B:

- Ingredient X: 17%
- Ingredient Y: 23%
- Ingredient Z: 60%

These contain the same ingredients and can proceed to the concentration comparison.

If Ingredient Z is replaced by a different non-fragrance substance in Formulation B, grouping generally fails unless an applicable regulatory provision permits the difference.

Pay particular attention to:

- Fragrances and perfume components.
- Colouring agents.
- Mixtures in mixtures (MiMs).
- Interchangeable component groups (ICGs).

Do not automatically approve an exception. Verify its specific legal requirements.

Rule 3 — Concentrations must fit the same permitted PCN reporting range

Components common to a proposed group must be capable of being reported using the same concentration or legally permitted concentration range.

Different exact concentrations do not automatically prevent grouping.

For each ingredient:

1. Find its minimum concentration across the proposed group.
2. Find its maximum concentration across the proposed group.
3. Determine its hazard classification.
4. Select the applicable PCN concentration-range table.
5. Determine whether one permitted reporting range can cover all actual concentrations.

There are two principal concentration categories.

Category A — Hazardous components of major concern

This includes components classified for at least one of the following:

- Acute toxicity Categories 1, 2 or 3.
- Specific target organ toxicity, single exposure, Categories 1 or 2.
- Specific target organ toxicity, repeated exposure, Categories 1 or 2.
- Skin corrosion Category 1, 1A, 1B or 1C.
- Serious eye damage Category 1.

Permitted maximum widths:

Concentration interval| Maximum reporting-range width
≥25% to <100%| 5 percentage points
≥10% to <25%| 3 percentage points
≥1% to <10%| 1 percentage point
≥0.1% to <1%| 0.3 percentage points
>0% to <0.1%| 0.1 percentage points

Category B — Other hazardous and non-hazardous components

Concentration interval| Maximum reporting-range width
≥25% to <100%| 20 percentage points
≥10% to <25%| 10 percentage points
≥1% to <10%| 3 percentage points
>0% to <1%| 1 percentage point

Critical calculation instructions:

- The permitted maximum width must be respected throughout the entire proposed reporting range.
- Where a proposed range crosses a concentration-band boundary, apply the stricter width applicable to any part of that range.
- Percentage-point differences are not relative percentage changes.
- Do not confuse these tables with Annex VIII Table 4, which concerns changes in concentrations previously notified as exact values.
- A common reporting range must cover every actual concentration of the component in the group.
- Do not arbitrarily round concentrations to make them fit.

Example:

Ingredient X is not a component of major concern.

- Formulation A: 5%
- Formulation B: 7%

The range 5–7% is 2 percentage points wide. The applicable maximum width is 3 percentage points.

Result: Pass.

If Formulation B instead contains 9%, the required 5–9% interval is 4 percentage points wide.

Result: Fail for that proposed grouping under the ordinary concentration-range rules.

If ingredient hazard classifications are missing, mark the concentration test as requiring verification rather than assuming the more permissive table applies.

Rule 4 — Fragrance / perfume exception

Annex VIII permits differences in perfume components between mixtures within the same group under specific conditions.

The total concentration of the differing perfume components must not exceed 5% in each formulation.

The 5% threshold relates to perfume components that are not present in every mixture in the proposed group.

Common perfume components present in all mixtures do not count towards that specific 5% threshold. Their reported concentrations must still comply with applicable requirements.

Example:

Formulation A:

- Identical base ingredients.
- Perfume A: 3%.

Formulation B:

- Identical base ingredients.
- Perfume B: 4%.

If all other grouping conditions are satisfied, grouping may be possible.

For every proposed group:

1. Identify perfume components present in every formulation.
2. Identify perfume components present in only some formulations.
3. Calculate the combined actual concentration of the differing perfume components in each formulation.
4. Confirm that no formulation exceeds 5%.
5. Record the identity and classification of the perfumes used in each formulation.

Do not assume that an ingredient qualifies as a perfume merely because its name suggests fragrance.

Some perfume components may be subject to special concentration-reporting provisions. These do not remove the need to verify the actual 5% threshold.

Rule 5 — Other component identification provisions

The regulation includes special provisions for:

- Generic component identifiers (GCIs), including eligible perfumes and colouring agents.
- Interchangeable component groups (ICGs).
- Certain special composition-reporting arrangements.

A generic component identifier for colouring agents is not automatically permitted for every dye. Its conditions must be verified, including permitted use, hazard classification and concentration limits.

Similarly, different ingredients must not automatically be classified as interchangeable.

If one of these provisions may allow grouping that would otherwise fail, mark the formulations as Special provision — review required and identify the relevant Annex VIII section.

Do not approve the grouping without evidence that the provision's conditions have been met.

4. Read and prepare the supplied data

Read every relevant worksheet in my Excel file.

Identify:

- Formulation name or ID.
- Ingredient name.
- CAS or EC number, if supplied.
- Ingredient concentration.
- Finished-mixture hazard classifications.
- Ingredient hazard classifications, if supplied.
- Ingredients identified as perfumes or colouring agents.
- MiM identifiers or UFIs, if supplied.

The data may be organised with formulations in rows or columns. Adapt to the actual structure.

Before comparing formulations:

1. Convert concentrations to a consistent percentage basis.
2. Identify whether concentrations are weight-based or volume-based.
3. Standardise ingredient identities.
4. Standardise the hazard classification notation.
5. Check for missing or duplicate ingredient entries.
6. Check whether concentrations total approximately 100%, allowing for justified rounding.

Keep a record of all changes made during data cleaning.

Never silently replace missing values with zero.

A missing concentration is unknown. An ingredient explicitly recorded at 0% is absent.

If the same ingredient appears multiple times within one formulation, combine the concentrations only when the entries genuinely refer to the same component.

5. Perform the grouping analysis

Step 1 — Classify formulations by finished-mixture hazard classification

Create sets of formulations with exactly matching health and physical hazard classifications.

Do not compare formulations for final grouping across incompatible hazard-classification sets.

Flag formulations that are unclassified or classified only for environmental hazards, because mandatory PCN scope is a separate question.

Step 2 — Compare component identities

Within each compatible hazard-classification set:

- Identify common components.
- Identify components that differ.
- Distinguish perfume differences from non-perfume differences.
- Flag uncertain component identities.

Determine whether the composition requirements can be met.

Step 3 — Compare concentrations

For every common component in each potential group:

Calculate:

- Minimum concentration.
- Maximum concentration.
- Required concentration span.
- Applicable permitted maximum reporting-range width.
- A proposed common reporting range, if one is legally possible.
- Pass, fail or verification required.

Use the classification of the ingredient, not the finished mixture, to select the applicable concentration table.

Step 4 — Apply perfume exceptions

Where perfume ingredients vary:

- Calculate the total concentration of differing perfume components in each formulation.
- Check the 5% threshold.
- Verify that all other components and classifications satisfy grouping requirements.

Step 5 — Construct proposed PCN groups

Identify which formulations can be included together.

Important: assess each complete group, not just individual pairs.

Two formulations may each be compatible with a third formulation without all three being compatible in one group.

For every proposed group, verify that every member satisfies all relevant conditions across the entire group.

Aim to minimise the number of separate submissions without creating groups that violate the rules.

Do not place the same formulation into multiple proposed groups in the final recommendation.

If there are several valid ways to partition the formulations, prefer the arrangement that uses the fewest defensible groups, and explain any material alternatives.

6. Required results

Provide the results in the following format.

Output A — Summary of proposed groups

Group ID| Formulations included| Number of formulations| Assessment| Explanation
Group 1| A, B, C| 3| Potentially groupable| Same classification, components and permitted ranges
Group 2| D, E| 2| Potentially groupable| Only permitted perfume differences
Ungrouped| F| 1| Cannot join Group 1 or 2| Different health hazard classification

These are illustrative examples, not results to assume.

Use these assessment categories:

- GROUPABLE: All relevant grouping conditions have been verified from the available evidence.
- CONDITIONALLY GROUPABLE: Appears eligible, but specified information or regulatory verification is outstanding.
- NOT GROUPABLE: A mandatory grouping condition is demonstrably not met.
- INSUFFICIENT DATA: Available information is inadequate to determine eligibility.

If the conditions for a special provision may apply but are not verified, use CONDITIONALLY GROUPABLE or INSUFFICIENT DATA as appropriate.

Output B — Component concentration comparison

For each proposed group, provide a table showing:

Ingredient| Minimum %| Maximum %| Applicable PCN category| Proposed common reporting range| Result

Include every component relevant to deciding the grouping.

Highlight concentration differences that prevent grouping.

Output C — Rejected grouping explanations

For every formulation that cannot be grouped with a candidate group, specify the exact reason.

Examples:

- Different finished-mixture health hazard classification.
- Different non-fragrance ingredient.
- Component concentrations cannot fit one permitted PCN reporting range.
- Differing perfumes exceed the 5% limit.
- Different legal submitter.
- Ingredient identity or hazard classification cannot be confirmed.

Identify the specific ingredient and values causing the failure wherever possible.

Output D — Outstanding information

List only the missing information necessary to confirm the assessment.

Examples:

- Ingredient-level CLP classification for CAS XXXXX.
- Identity of an unspecified perfume.
- Confirmation that two supplier ingredients represent the same MiM.
- Verification of eligibility under an interchangeable component group.
- Confirmation that formulations are placed on the market by the same legal submitter.

Do not request information that is already available in the uploaded file.

Output E — Final recommendation

Provide:

1. Total formulations analysed.
2. Number of proposed PCN groups.
3. Number of formulations potentially eligible for grouping.
4. Number of formulations that cannot be included in the proposed groups.
5. Number requiring additional information.
6. The recommended group membership for each formulation.

If calculations are possible, provide the actual results rather than merely explaining the method.

7. Accuracy and quality requirements

- Analyse all formulations, not just examples.
- Do not infer missing ingredient classifications from finished-mixture classifications.
- Do not treat identical H-statements as proof that compositions can be grouped.
- Do not treat different concentrations as an automatic grouping failure.
- Do not assume perfumes, colourants or interchangeable ingredients qualify for exemptions without verification.
- Do not confuse PCN concentration reporting ranges with SDS reporting ranges or composition-change update tolerances.
- Do not group formulations with different health or physical hazard classifications.
- Do not merge formulations based only on similar trade names or commercial purposes.
- Do not describe a proposed group as legally confirmed if important information remains missing.
- Preserve original formulation IDs so the findings can be traced to the source file.
- Cite the applicable regulatory provisions when explaining significant exclusions or exceptions.

Distinguish clearly between the technical grouping assessment and the separate task of creating and submitting a PCN dossier.

Assume for the initial technical assessment that all formulations belong to the same legal submitter unless the supplied data indicates otherwise. Flag this assumption for confirmation.

8. Final instruction

Your priority is to identify exactly which formulations can be grouped and why.

Perform the full analysis using my uploaded file.

Begin by explaining how you interpreted the spreadsheet, then present the grouping results and component-level evidence.

Do not give only a generic explanation of PCN regulations.

Do not stop at matching the hazard classifications.

If the data supports a preliminary assessment but not a confirmed conclusion, complete the preliminary assessment and clearly identify what remains unverified.

Where practical, create a downloadable Excel assessment with:

- A formulation summary sheet.
- Proposed PCN groups.
- Component concentration comparisons.
- Excluded formulations and reasons.
- Outstanding verification items.

Use clear, concise regulatory language, with results that can be checked manually by a chemical regulatory professional.
